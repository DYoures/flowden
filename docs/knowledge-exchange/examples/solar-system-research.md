# The Solar System

A concise, interconnected research synthesis of our Solar System. It moves from how it formed and how it is organized, through the Sun, the eight planets, dwarf planets, moons and small-body populations, out to the edge of the Sun's influence, and finishes with exploration history and unresolved questions. Figures are rounded and drawn from NASA and peer-reviewed sources listed at the end.

---
## Note: Solar System Overview
folder: 01 Foundations
tags: overview, introduction, astronomy

The Solar System is the Sun plus everything gravitationally bound to it: eight planets, their moons, five officially recognized dwarf planets, and countless asteroids, comets and icy bodies. It formed about 4.57 billion years ago [src-3] and contains one star, which holds more than 99% of the system's total mass (see [[The Sun]]).

The system is commonly divided into:
- The **inner system**: four rocky worlds, see [[Terrestrial Planets]].
- The **asteroid belt**, see [[Asteroid Belt]].
- The **outer system**: two gas giants and two ice giants, see [[Gas Giants]] and [[Ice Giants]].
- The **distant reservoirs**: [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]] and [[Comets & the Oort Cloud]].

For origins, read [[Formation & Age of the Solar System]]. For distances and layout, read [[Scale, Structure & Regions]]. For a numbers table, see [[Planetary Quick-Reference Data]].

## Note: Formation & Age of the Solar System
folder: 01 Foundations
tags: formation, nebular-hypothesis, protoplanetary-disk, age

The leading explanation is the **nebular hypothesis**: a cloud of gas and dust collapsed under gravity, most likely triggered by a disturbance such as a nearby supernova shockwave. Most material fell into the center to form the proto-Sun, while the rest flattened into a rotating **protoplanetary disk**.

Within the disk, dust grains stuck together into pebbles, then planetesimals, then protoplanets. Near the young Sun, heat allowed only rock and metal to remain solid, producing small terrestrial planets. Beyond the **frost line** (roughly where water ice can survive), ices were abundant, so cores grew large enough to capture gas, producing giant planets.

**Age:** The oldest dated solids in meteorites, calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions, give an age of about 4.568 billion years, which is the standard anchor for the age of the Solar System [src-3].

The Moon likely formed from a giant impact between early Earth and a Mars-sized body, a model supported by simulations [src-12]. See also [[Moons & Ocean Worlds]].

Later rearrangement of the planets is covered in [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]].

## Note: Planetary Migration & Early Evolution
folder: 01 Foundations
tags: migration, nice-model, grand-tack, dynamics

Modern models show the planets did not stay exactly where they formed.

- **Grand Tack:** Jupiter may have migrated inward and then back outward early in the disk's life, which could explain why Mars is small and why the asteroid belt has mixed compositions [src-6].
- **Nice model:** After the gas disk vanished, interactions between the giant planets and a disk of icy planetesimals may have shifted the giants' orbits, scattering small bodies both inward and outward [src-5]. This helps explain the architecture of the giant planets and the populations of the [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]] and [[Asteroid Belt]].

These are models, not direct observations, and details are still debated.

## Note: Scale, Structure & Regions
folder: 01 Foundations
tags: distances, astronomical-unit, structure

The standard distance unit is the **astronomical unit (AU)**, the mean Earth-Sun distance, about 149.6 million km [src-2].

| Region | Approximate distance from Sun |
|---|---|
| Mercury to Mars (inner system) | 0.39 to 1.52 AU |
| Asteroid belt | about 2.1 to 3.3 AU |
| Jupiter to Neptune | 5.2 to 30.1 AU |
| Kuiper belt | about 30 to 50 AU |
| Heliopause (edge of Sun's wind bubble) | about 120 AU |
| Oort cloud (hypothesized) | thousands to ~100,000 AU |

Light from the Sun takes about 8 minutes to reach Earth and several hours to reach Neptune. Nearly all planets orbit in roughly the same plane and direction as the Sun's rotation, a signature of formation from a flat disk (see [[Formation & Age of the Solar System]]).

Related: [[Asteroid Belt]], [[Heliosphere & Interstellar Boundary]], [[Comets & the Oort Cloud]].

## Note: The Sun
folder: 02 The Sun & Planets
tags: sun, star, fusion, heliophysics

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star (G2V). Key facts [src-1][src-2]:
- Diameter: about 1.39 million km, roughly 109 times Earth's.
- Mass: more than 99.8% of the entire Solar System.
- Core temperature: about 15 million K; visible surface (photosphere): about 5,800 K.
- Energy source: hydrogen fusing into helium in the core.
- Age: about 4.6 billion years, roughly halfway through its main-sequence life.

The Sun emits the **solar wind**, a stream of charged particles that inflates the heliosphere (see [[Heliosphere & Interstellar Boundary]]). Its activity follows an approximately 11-year cycle of sunspots, flares and coronal mass ejections that can affect Earth's technology.

In several billion years it will swell into a red giant, then shed its outer layers and leave a white dwarf.

Its gravity organizes everything else: see [[Solar System Overview]] and [[Terrestrial Planets]].

## Note: Terrestrial Planets
folder: 02 The Sun & Planets
tags: mercury, venus, earth, mars, rocky-planets

The four inner planets are small, rocky and dense, with solid surfaces and few or no moons [src-1][src-2].

**Mercury** (0.39 AU): the smallest planet, about 4,879 km across, with a very thin exosphere and extreme day-night temperature swings. It orbits the Sun in 88 days and has no moons.

**Venus** (0.72 AU): similar in size to Earth (about 12,104 km) but with a dense carbon-dioxide atmosphere and a runaway greenhouse effect, giving surface temperatures near 465 C, hot enough to melt lead. It rotates very slowly and backward (retrograde). No moons.

**Earth** (1.00 AU): about 12,742 km across, the only known world with liquid surface water and life, and one large moon.

**Mars** (1.52 AU): about 6,779 km across, with a thin CO2 atmosphere, polar ice caps, evidence of ancient rivers and lakes, and Olympus Mons, the tallest known volcano in the Solar System. It has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos.

Why they are small: see [[Formation & Age of the Solar System]] and the Grand Tack discussion in [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]]. Compare with the outer worlds in [[Gas Giants]]. Moons: [[Moons & Ocean Worlds]].

## Note: Gas Giants
folder: 02 The Sun & Planets
tags: jupiter, saturn, gas-giants, rings

Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface [src-1][src-2].

**Jupiter** (5.2 AU): the largest planet, about 139,800 km across and over 300 Earth masses (more than twice all other planets combined). It has banded clouds, the centuries-old Great Red Spot storm, a strong magnetic field and a faint ring system. Orbital period: about 11.9 years.

**Saturn** (9.58 AU): about 116,500 km across, with the most spectacular ring system, made mostly of water-ice particles. Its average density is lower than water's. Orbital period: about 29.4 years.

Both have very large moon systems, including Ganymede (the largest moon in the Solar System), Europa, Io, Callisto, Titan and Enceladus; see [[Moons & Ocean Worlds]]. Jupiter's gravity shapes the [[Asteroid Belt]] and may have migrated early on (see [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]]).

Compare: [[Ice Giants]].

## Note: Ice Giants
folder: 02 The Sun & Planets
tags: uranus, neptune, ice-giants

Uranus and Neptune have thick interiors rich in water, ammonia and methane "ices" (in hot, dense fluid form) beneath hydrogen-helium atmospheres [src-1][src-2]. Methane absorbs red light and gives both their blue-green color.

**Uranus** (19.2 AU): about 50,700 km across, about 14.5 Earth masses. Its rotation axis is tilted about 98 degrees, so it effectively rolls around the Sun on its side. Orbital period: about 84 years. Discovered in 1781 by William Herschel.

**Neptune** (30.1 AU): about 49,200 km across, about 17 Earth masses. It has the fastest winds measured on any planet, over 2,000 km/h, and an orbital period of about 165 years. Its existence was predicted mathematically before it was observed (1846).

Only Voyager 2 has visited them (Uranus in 1986, Neptune in 1989); see [[Space Exploration Milestones]]. Neptune's largest moon Triton is discussed in [[Moons & Ocean Worlds]], and Neptune's outer neighborhood in [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]].

Compare: [[Gas Giants]].

## Note: Dwarf Planets & the IAU Definition
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: dwarf-planets, pluto, iau, definition

In 2006 the International Astronomical Union defined a **planet** as a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) is massive enough to be nearly round, and (3) has cleared its orbital neighborhood. A **dwarf planet** meets the first two criteria but not the third, and is not a moon [src-4].

Five are officially recognized:
- **Ceres**: in the [[Asteroid Belt]], about 940 km across.
- **Pluto**: about 2,377 km across, with five known moons. Visited by New Horizons in 2015 [src-16].
- **Eris**: about as large as Pluto but more massive; its discovery in 2005 triggered the redefinition.
- **Haumea** and **Makemake**: icy bodies in the outer Solar System.

All but Ceres lie in the [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]] region. Many more candidates likely exist. Pluto was reclassified under this definition, which remains debated by some planetary scientists.

See also [[Solar System Overview]].

## Note: Moons & Ocean Worlds
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: moons, europa, enceladus, titan, astrobiology

Moons are natural satellites. Counts keep rising as telescopes improve. NASA states the Solar System has "more than 890 moons" including those of dwarf planets and asteroids [src-14]. Among the planets, recent tallies list Mercury and Venus 0, Earth 1, Mars 2, Jupiter about 95 or more, Saturn 274 or more, Uranus 28 to 29, and Neptune 16 [src-13][src-14]. These are moving numbers; check NASA for the latest.

Notable moons:
- **Earth's Moon**: likely formed by a giant impact [src-12].
- **Ganymede** (Jupiter): the largest moon, bigger than Mercury, with its own magnetic field.
- **Io** (Jupiter): the most volcanically active body known.
- **Europa** (Jupiter): a global subsurface saltwater ocean beneath an ice shell.
- **Titan** (Saturn): the only moon with a thick atmosphere, with methane lakes and seas.
- **Enceladus** (Saturn): water-vapor plumes from a subsurface ocean.
- **Triton** (Neptune): orbits backward, so it was probably captured from the Kuiper belt.

"Ocean worlds" such as Europa, Enceladus, Titan and Ganymede are prime targets in the search for life beyond Earth; see [[Space Exploration Milestones]].

Parent planets: [[Gas Giants]], [[Ice Giants]], [[Terrestrial Planets]].

## Note: Asteroid Belt
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: asteroids, main-belt, ceres, vesta

The main asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter at roughly 2.1 to 3.3 AU. It holds millions of rocky and metallic bodies, yet its total mass is only a small fraction of the Moon's (roughly 3%) [src-1]. The space between objects is vast, so spacecraft pass through easily.

- The belt is the leftover of planetesimals that never merged into a planet, largely because of Jupiter's gravity.
- **Ceres** is the largest object and a dwarf planet (see [[Dwarf Planets & the IAU Definition]]); **Vesta** is among the largest asteroids. NASA's Dawn mission orbited both.
- Jupiter's orbital resonances carve empty lanes called **Kirkwood gaps**.
- Some asteroids cross Earth's orbit (near-Earth objects) and are tracked for impact risk.
- Sample-return missions such as Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS-REx have brought back material from asteroids Ryugu and Bennu.

Related: [[Gas Giants]], [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]], [[Space Exploration Milestones]].

## Note: Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: kuiper-belt, tnos, pluto, arrokoth, scattered-disc

Beyond Neptune lies a wide ring of icy bodies called the **Kuiper belt**, spanning about 30 to 50 AU. The first object after Pluto was found in 1992 [src-7]. Objects beyond Neptune are called **trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs)**.

- Home to dwarf planets including Pluto, Eris, Haumea and Makemake (see [[Dwarf Planets & the IAU Definition]]).
- The **scattered disc** contains objects on stretched, tilted orbits, and detached objects such as Sedna and 2012 VP113 hint at unseen influences [src-15]; see [[Planet Nine Hypothesis]].
- NASA's New Horizons flew past Pluto in 2015 and the small object Arrokoth in 2019, the most distant object explored up close at that time.
- Many short-period comets originate here (see [[Comets & the Oort Cloud]]).

The belt's structure is thought to record the giant planets' early migration [src-5]; see [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]].

## Note: Comets & the Oort Cloud
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: comets, oort-cloud, long-period-comets

**Comets** are icy bodies that develop a glowing coma and tails when heated near the Sun. Short-period comets (like Halley's, about 76 years) come largely from the [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]] region. Long-period comets arrive from all directions, which led Jan Oort in 1950 to propose a spherical shell of icy bodies surrounding the Solar System, the **Oort cloud** [src-8].

- Estimated extent: from a few thousand to around 100,000 AU, possibly reaching toward the nearest stars' regions.
- It has **not been directly observed**; it is inferred from comet orbits.
- It may hold trillions of icy bodies, perturbed inward by passing stars or galactic tides.

This is the outermost proposed boundary of the Sun's gravitational domain, far beyond the [[Heliosphere & Interstellar Boundary]]. Compare with [[Scale, Structure & Regions]].

## Note: Heliosphere & Interstellar Boundary
folder: 03 Small Bodies & Moons
tags: heliosphere, heliopause, voyager, interstellar-space

The **heliosphere** is the bubble inflated by the solar wind around the Sun. The solar wind slows abruptly at the **termination shock**, then the **heliopause** marks where the Sun's wind gives way to the interstellar medium.

- Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause in 2012 at about 121 AU, the first human-made object in interstellar space [src-9].
- Voyager 2 crossed in 2018 at about 119 AU, with instruments that measured the plasma directly [src-10].
- The heliosphere shields the inner Solar System from a share of galactic cosmic rays.

Note the difference: the heliopause is the edge of the Sun's *wind*, while the Oort cloud (see [[Comets & the Oort Cloud]]) lies much farther out and marks the edge of the Sun's *gravitational* reach. Distances: [[Scale, Structure & Regions]]. Probes: [[Space Exploration Milestones]]. Solar wind source: [[The Sun]].

## Note: Planet Nine Hypothesis
folder: 05 Open Questions
tags: planet-nine, hypothesis, unsolved, trans-neptunian

Some distant TNOs have orbits that seem clustered in unexpected ways. In 2016, Batygin and Brown proposed that an undiscovered planet, several times Earth's mass on a very distant orbit, could explain this [src-11]. Earlier work on Sedna-like objects had also suggested an unseen perturber [src-15].

Status: **unconfirmed**. No direct detection exists, and other researchers argue the clustering could come from observational bias or other explanations. It remains an active, debated hypothesis rather than an established fact.

Context: [[Kuiper Belt & Trans-Neptunian Objects]] and [[Planetary Migration & Early Evolution]].

## Note: Space Exploration Milestones
folder: 04 Exploration
tags: exploration, missions, nasa, voyager

Selected milestones:
- **1957**: Sputnik 1 begins the space age.
- **1962**: Mariner 2 makes the first successful planetary flyby (Venus).
- **1969**: Apollo 11 lands humans on the Moon (see [[Moons & Ocean Worlds]]).
- **1973 to 1979**: Pioneer 10 and 11 reach Jupiter and Saturn.
- **1977**: Voyager 1 and 2 launch; Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to visit Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989); see [[Ice Giants]].
- **1995 to 2003**: Galileo orbits Jupiter.
- **2004 to 2017**: Cassini orbits Saturn; its Huygens probe lands on Titan in 2005.
- **2012 and 2018**: Voyagers cross into interstellar space ([[Heliosphere & Interstellar Boundary]]).
- **2015**: New Horizons flies past Pluto [src-16].
- **2011 and 2015**: Dawn orbits Vesta and Ceres ([[Asteroid Belt]]).
- **2021**: Perseverance rover lands on Mars ([[Terrestrial Planets]]).
- **2023 and 2024**: ESA's JUICE and NASA's Europa Clipper launch toward Jupiter's icy moons.

Active exploration of the Sun by Parker Solar Probe studies the corona ([[The Sun]]). Missions change often, so check NASA for current status.
